Cryptography & Network Security · course project · proof of concept

An SOS that finds its own way out.

Jilla is a women-safety SOS for Android that still works when there is no signal and no internet. The alert hops phone to phone over Bluetooth until any phone with a connection hands it to the authority. Every phone in between carries it without being able to read it, change it or fake it.

01 · The problem

Help is one call away, if the call goes through.

India's emergency system is real and growing: 112 is live in all 36 states and UTs and handled about 10.89 crore calls in FY 2022–23. Every channel it offers assumes the person in danger has a working network at that moment.

Scale

4.48 lakh recorded crimes a year

NCRB's 2023 data: 4,48,211 crimes against women, a rate of 66.2 per lakh women. Kidnapping and abduction (88,605) and assault to outrage modesty (83,891) are crimes where minutes matter.

Coverage

Dead zones are where it's worst

Rural teledensity is 58.8% against 125.3% urban. Thousands of villages still lack 4G, and phones also go dark in basements, lifts, highways, crowded events and on low-balance SIMs.

Shutdowns

Networks get switched off

India had 65 internet shutdowns in 2025 and 84 in 2024, the most of any democracy, often during protests and unrest, when people are least safe.

Figures are from NCRB's Crime in India 2023 (released 30 Sep 2025), a Rajya Sabha reply on ERSS-112 (Dec 2023), Ookla's H1 2025 analysis and Access Now's shutdown reports, each linked in the news chapter.

03 · Why we built it

Remove the single point of failure.

Every SOS app we looked at depends on one thing being up at the worst moment: the victim's own connection. We set out to make that dependency optional, without turning strangers' phones into a privacy or spoofing risk.

Purpose

Get the alert out, whatever the network is doing

If the victim's phone has internet, it delivers directly. If not, any nearby phone running Jilla becomes the path, and if nobody is nearby, phones carry the alert until someone is. The alert keeps moving until it reaches help.

Constraint

Strangers must carry it blind

Relays are random people. They must not learn who is in danger or exactly where, must not be able to tamper with or forge alerts, and must not be able to track the victim. The cryptography does that work, so the network doesn't need to be trusted.

Gap we saw

Existing tools stop at the network

Calling 112, SOS shortcuts, location-sharing apps and messaging all need signal or data on the victim's phone. Bluetooth mesh chat apps work offline, but they're for messaging, with no authority, no verification and no privacy from relays.

Goal

A feature Android could ship

Built as a proof of concept for a system-level feature: on every phone by default, a mesh that already exists on the day someone needs it. That network effect is what makes mesh delivery realistic.

05 · Delivery paths

Four ways out, tried at once.

The phone doesn't choose a route. It tries all of them in parallel, and the server keeps exactly one copy of each update.

Direct

Victim's phone is online

It uploads the sealed packet straight to the authority, sends SMS to emergency contacts from the victim's own number, and broadcasts to the mesh as well.

Gateway

Someone nearby is online

A bystander's phone receives the alert over Bluetooth and uploads it. It can't read what it's uploading. The laptop authority can also receive straight over Web Bluetooth.

Multi-hop

Nobody nearby is online

Phones re-broadcast to their neighbours, one Bluetooth hop at a time (tens of metres each), with a hop limit of 7 and duplicate detection on every phone.

Carry-forward

Nobody nearby at all

Phones keep the alert and re-broadcast it every 10 seconds for the relay window (2 minutes in the demo, an hour or more in a real rollout), so it moves with people until it meets a path out.

06 · Crypto & security

Trust the math, not the messenger.

Every packet has four parts. Only the hop counter can change in transit; everything else is signed, and the private part is sealed so only the authority can open it.

07 · Why it's a CNS project

Cryptography and network security, end to end.

Jilla is an exercise in building a secure protocol over an untrusted, delay-tolerant network. Every relay is treated as a Dolev–Yao adversary: it can read, drop, delay, replay, reorder and inject packets. The design has to hold anyway.

Confidentiality

Hybrid public-key encryption (ECDH + stream cipher) seals personal data to the authority.

Integrity

Ed25519 over the packet plus Poly1305 inside the seal: any changed bit is rejected.

Authenticity

Two-level signatures: one-time incident key outside, registered identity key inside.

Availability

Mesh flooding, carry-forward and rate limits keep alerts moving under loss and attack.

Public-key cryptography

Three key types, kept separate

Identity key (Ed25519, long-term): registered once, proves who the user is to the authority only. Incident key (Ed25519, one per SOS): signs the public packet, then is discarded. Authority key (X25519): the only key that can open sealed blocks; bundled into the app and pinned. Signing and encryption never share a key.

Authenticated encryption

The sealed box, byte for byte

Anonymous sealed box (libsodium crypto_box_seal): a fresh ephemeral X25519 key per seal, ECDH with the authority key, HSalsa20 to derive the key, BLAKE2b to derive the nonce, XSalsa20-Poly1305 to encrypt and authenticate. We implemented it on BouncyCastle and verified it matches PyNaCl exactly.

# sealed block (per authority key, 48 B overhead)
epk, esk  ← X25519.keygen()                      fresh every seal
s          = X25519(esk, authority_pk)
k          = HSalsa20(s, 0¹⁶)                       crypto_box_beforenm
n          = BLAKE2b-192(epk ‖ authority_pk)        no nonce reuse: epk is unique
sealed     = epk ‖ Poly1305-tag ‖ XSalsa20(k, n) ⊕ details

# outer signature (relays verify, nobody can forge)
sig        = Ed25519.sign(incident_sk, header ‖ sealed)
packet     = CBOR[ ttl, header, sealed, sig ]       ttl unsigned, like IPv4

# inside the seal: ties this one-time key to a registered person
binding    = Ed25519.sign(identity_sk, "SOSMESH-BIND-v1" ‖ incident_pk)   domain-separated

# registration: proof of possession, fields bound, replay-bounded
reg_sig    = Ed25519.sign(identity_sk, "JILLA-REG-v2" ‖ ts ‖ phone ‖ device ‖ name ‖ identity_pk)
Security engineering choices

Decisions we can defend

No forward secrecy, on purpose: the authority must still decrypt an alert after the victim's phone is destroyed, so we use a one-way seal instead of an interactive key exchange. Unsigned TTL: relays must decrement it without the sender's key; the worst a relay can do is raise it to 15, and dedup still bounds the flood. Domain separation: every signature carries a context tag, so a signature from one protocol step can't be replayed in another. Constant-time MAC comparison when opening seals.

Network security

The network side is half the project

Flooding with a TTL and per-node duplicate caches, the classic broadcast-storm defence. Delay-tolerant networking: store-carry-forward with a bounded carry window. Bluetooth LE GATT: every phone is both server and client; packets (~500 B) are chunked to the negotiated MTU (capped at 512 B per write) with a length-prefixed framing. Defence in depth: HTTPS tunnel for gateways, token-protected dashboard, rate limits on unverified traffic.

08 · Architecture

One protocol core, four places it runs.

The crypto, packet format and relay engine are one plain Kotlin library. The same code runs in the app, in unit tests and in headless test nodes, and the transport can change from TCP to Bluetooth without touching it.

Android app

Kotlin · Jetpack Compose

  • Hold-to-trigger SOS, live location every 30 s
  • Bluetooth LE: GATT server + scanner
  • Foreground service keeps the mesh alive
  • SMS to contacts, nearby-alert notifications
Protocol core

Kotlin · BouncyCastle

  • Ed25519, X25519, sealed box
  • CBOR packet codec, replay guard
  • Relay engine: verify, dedup, TTL, carry
  • Transport interface: TCP or BLE
Authority server

Python · FastAPI · PyNaCl

  • Verify, dedup, decrypt, dispatch
  • Signed registration, SQLite
  • Live console with maps
  • Web Bluetooth receiver in the browser
Dev tools

Testing harness

  • mesh-sim: TCP hub with a "who can hear whom" graph
  • Headless JVM nodes
  • Debug-only adb hooks
  • Cloudflare tunnel for remote gateways

10 · What we tested

Every claim has a test behind it.

22 Kotlin unit tests and 14 backend tests, plus end-to-end runs on two physical phones over adb (a CMF Phone 1 and a Realme).

PASS

Libsodium interoperability

Kotlin opens PyNaCl-sealed boxes and matches its Ed25519 signatures byte for byte; PyNaCl verifies and opens Kotlin-built packets.

PASS

Every single-bit flip is rejected

Each bit of the header, sealed block and signature was flipped in turn; all were rejected. Every byte of the sealed box was also tampered with.

PASS

Replay, stale, forged and hijacked alerts

Repeated and older counters, out-of-window timestamps, signatures by other keys, fake binding signatures and alert_id hijacks are all refused.

PASS

Registration can't be abused

Registering someone else's key, swapping the phone number after signing, and replaying an old registration all fail.

PASS

Unverified alerts are capped, verified ones aren't

During a simulated flood of 15 unregistered alerts, 10 were dispatched as UNVERIFIED, 5 got 429, and a registered user's alert still went through.

PASS

Offline phone → Bluetooth → online phone → server

On real hardware: the CMF, with no route to the server, sent its SOS over Bluetooth; the Realme relayed it, showed "Someone nearby needs help" and uploaded it; the server decrypted the name, number and a location accurate to ±15 m.

11 · Limits

What this prototype can't do yet.

Mesh reach depends on people

Each Bluetooth hop is tens of metres. With nobody in range, the alert waits on the victim's phone until someone comes near. Wide adoption or fixed relay "poles" are what make this reliable.

Within one incident, updates are linkable

A bystander can tell that several updates belong to the same alert. Across incidents, alerts are unlinkable.

The authority is a stub

The server simulates dispatch to 112 and contacts. It is not connected to any real emergency service, and must not be relied on in a real emergency.

Prototype-grade key storage

The identity key lives in app-private storage. A production build should keep it in the Android Keystore, and the dashboard needs real authentication instead of a link token.

12 · Roadmap

From app to Android feature.

The mesh is only as good as the number of phones in it. Built into the OS, every Android phone becomes a relay by default.

Done: protocol, server and Bluetooth mesh

Signed and sealed packets, signed registration, gateway upload, store-carry-forward, phone-to-phone BLE and Web Bluetooth to the authority laptop.

Next: survive restarts

Persist the active SOS and carried alerts so a crash, update or reboot never ends an alert silently.

Next: system triggers

Power button pressed five times, a Quick Settings tile, and integration with Android's existing Emergency SOS flow.

Later: a relay service in the OS

A low-power Bluetooth relay running in Play services, so phones relay without anyone installing an app.

Later: fixed poles and real dispatch

Solar Bluetooth poles in coverage gaps, and integration with ERSS-112 with proper key management.
